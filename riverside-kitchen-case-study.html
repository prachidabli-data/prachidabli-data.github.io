Objective

The problem

An independent restaurant lives or dies on small margins. Success means understanding both halves of the business at once: what customers buy and when (demand), and what it costs to source and stock the ingredients behind it (supply). The Riverside Kitchen project answers five questions that together cover that full picture:

  • Demand patterns — when is the restaurant busy, by day, service, and season?
  • Menu performance — which dishes are stars, and which quietly lose money?
  • Customer behaviour — how much do people spend, in what group sizes, and when?
  • Suppliers & cost — who is reliable, and where does the ingredient budget go?
  • Inventory & waste — where does stock get wasted, and why?
The Data

A realistic synthetic dataset

This project uses a synthetic dataset generated in Python to realistically mirror the structure and behaviour of a real restaurant's operations. Synthetic data is used because real operational data of this kind is commercially sensitive and not publicly available, allowing the analytical approach to be demonstrated end-to-end without confidentiality concerns.

Realistic patterns were deliberately built in: busier weekends and dinner service, a festive December peak and quiet January, menu items spread across the four menu-engineering quadrants, suppliers with differing reliability, and higher waste on perishable ingredients. The data is organised as an 8-table star schema.

Methodology

From raw data to insight

The analysis was led in Python (pandas) and independently reproduced in SQL (MySQL), validating every finding across two tools. The core pandas pattern throughout was merge → group → summarise: joining fact and dimension tables, grouping, and aggregating.

1 · Demand patterns

Orders peak hard at the weekend (Saturday busiest, Monday quietest) and dinner drives the business, around two-thirds of orders and a higher spend per head than lunch. Revenue shows clear seasonality, peaking every December and troughing in January.

2 · Menu engineering

Every dish was classified by popularity (units sold) and profitability (margin) into Stars, Plowhorses, Puzzles, and Dogs. The Riverside Burger, Fish & Chips, House Cocktail, Roast Chicken, and Seafood Linguine are Stars; high-margin Ribeye Steak and Wild Mushroom Risotto are under-selling Puzzles worth promoting; low-performing Dogs are candidates for review.

3 · Customer behaviour

Parties of two dominate. Solo diners spend the most per head (~£17.40 vs a flat ~£15.30 for larger groups), and dinner generates roughly double the revenue of lunch. Average spend per head is £15.63.

4 · Suppliers & cost

An OTIF (On-Time-In-Full) scorecard ranked suppliers from City Drinks (~95%) down to Coastal Seafood (~71%). The key tension: Coastal Seafood is both the least reliable supplier and the largest single spend — the business depends most on the partner it can trust least. Total ingredient spend was £541,908.

5 · Inventory & waste

Perishable ingredients waste around four times more than non-perishables (~10% vs ~2.5%), concentrated in fresh seafood and produce — directly linking waste back to the same volatile seafood supply chain seen in Question 4.

Validation & Problem-Solving

When the numbers looked wrong

An analysis is only as trustworthy as its checks. At several points the output looked plausible at a glance but failed a sense-check — and tracing each one back to its cause was where most of the real work happened. Three examples from this build:

Tableau · date aggregation

A “peak” month that was too big to be real

Problem

The revenue trend first showed June as the highest month at roughly £188,000.

Diagnosis

No single month in the source data exceeded about £109,000, so £188k was impossible. Tableau was treating the date as a discrete month name, collapsing both Junes (2024 and 2025) into one bar and summing them — which also masked the genuine December festive peak.

Fix

Switched the date to a continuous month so all 18 months plot individually, then cross-checked the monthly totals against the SQL layer, treated as the ground-truth source.

-- SQL ground truth: true monthly revenue, used to validate the dashboard
SELECT d.year, d.month, ROUND(SUM(o.order_total)) AS revenue
FROM fact_orders o JOIN dim_date d ON o.date_id = d.date_id
GROUP BY d.year, d.month ORDER BY revenue DESC;

What it shows: distrusting a figure that is “too big to be true,” and validating a BI tool against a verified SQL source rather than assuming the dashboard is correct.

SUM vs AVG · additivity

A waste rate of 7,000%

Problem

The waste visual’s values ran from 0 to about 7,000 instead of a believable 2–10%.

Diagnosis

A percentage can’t exceed 100% per snapshot, so 7,000 was a clear logic error. The percentage was being summed across hundreds of inventory snapshots — but a rate must be averaged, never added up.

Fix

Switched the aggregation to an average, matching the SQL definition, which already averaged the rate.

-- a rate is averaged, never summed
ROUND(AVG(inv.waste_pct), 1) AS avg_waste_pct

What it shows: knowing which measures are additive and which are not, and using a quick plausibility check on the output range to catch an aggregation mistake.

MySQL · data loading

A table that imported zero rows

Problem

Importing dim_date into MySQL finished “successfully” but loaded 0 of 547 rows — every row silently rejected.

Diagnosis

The is_weekend column holds the text values “Yes”/“No”. The import wizard had inferred a numeric column type, so every text value failed the type check and the whole load failed.

Fix

Defined the flag columns explicitly as VARCHAR in the schema before loading, so the text imports cleanly and is handled as text in queries.

CREATE TABLE dim_date (
    ...
    is_weekend  VARCHAR(5)   -- "Yes"/"No", not a number
);
-- dim_ingredient.perishable handled the same way

What it shows: reading import behaviour carefully and designing the schema to match the real shape of the data rather than trusting type inference.

Recommendations

What the business should have done — and should do now

Analysis only matters if it drives action. Split into the decisions that should have been made at the outset, and the moves the data supports today:

At the start

  • Dual-source perishables from day one. Given how exposed fresh seafood is, a backup seafood supplier should have been onboarded at launch to de-risk the single biggest, least reliable spend.
  • Engineer the menu at launch. Price dishes against their margin tier rather than flat markups, so high-cost items like Fish & Chips weren't left as thin-margin Plowhorses.
  • Build seasonality into stock planning. The December peak and January trough are predictable; ordering and staffing should have flexed seasonally from the outset.
  • Set perishable par levels early. Demand-linked ordering rules would have curbed the ~10% perishable waste before it became habit.

Right now

  • Promote the Puzzles. Give high-margin, under-selling dishes (Ribeye, Risotto) better menu placement and specials to lift their volume.
  • Nudge Plowhorse prices. Small increases on popular low-margin items (wine, beer, sides) flow almost entirely to profit.
  • Renegotiate or replace Coastal Seafood. Address the reliability-vs-spend tension directly through dual-sourcing or terms.
  • Cut perishable waste. Tighten seafood and produce ordering to demand; even halving the gap meaningfully improves food-cost margin.
  • Lean into dinner and weekends. The highest-value service and days deserve the marketing and capacity focus.
