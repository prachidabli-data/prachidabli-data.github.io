Objective

The problem

A refinery moves two very different products through its supply chain: High Speed Diesel (HSD), a high-volume product sold to a broad base of fuel stations, transport fleets, and industrial clients; and Aviation Turbine Fuel (ATF), a lower-volume, higher-sensitivity product sold to a small, concentrated set of airports and airlines. Behind both sits a flow of inbound crude arriving from international suppliers.

Managing this chain requires clear visibility over five questions:

  • Volume and demand — how much crude is coming in and how much product is going out, to whom, and how is it trending?
  • Supplier and transporter reliability — who delivers on time and in full, and who causes problems?
  • Logistics and delivery efficiency — where do delays build up, and which routes underperform?
  • Cost — what does it cost to move each product, and where is money being lost?
  • Inventory health — are stock levels healthy, and where is there stockout risk?

The objective was to build an end-to-end analytics solution that answers these questions and turns raw logistics data into decisions a refinery operations team can act on.

The Data

A realistic synthetic dataset

The project uses a synthetic dataset built to mirror the structure and behaviour of real refinery logistics operations. It was generated programmatically in Python, with realistic patterns deliberately built in:

  • Crude measured in barrels, refined products in litres, reflecting real industry practice.
  • Seasonality in demand (ATF peaks in summer and December; HSD lifts slightly in winter).
  • Reliability profiles that differ by supplier and transporter, so performance analysis surfaces real differences.
  • Cost and price drift month to month, and occasional short deliveries, providing genuine data-quality issues to clean.

The data is organised as a star schema of nine tables — six dimensions (product, supplier, customer, transporter, location, date) and three facts (inbound crude shipments, outbound product deliveries, monthly inventory snapshots).

Methodology

From raw data to insight

Step 1 — Database & schema design (SQL)

The foundation is a relational database, oil_supply_chain, built in MySQL as a star schema. Six dimension tables hold descriptive reference data; three fact tables hold the measurable events at the centre of the analysis. Each table uses appropriate data types, with primary keys identifying every row and foreign keys linking facts back to dimensions to enforce data integrity. The schema is written to be re-runnable, rebuilding cleanly at any point.

Demonstrates: relational data modelling, star-schema design, and data integrity.

Step 2 — Loading the data & resolving a data-type issue

The nine CSV files were loaded via MySQL Workbench's Import Wizard, dimensions first then facts so foreign keys were respected. During the load, dim_date returned 0 records imported while every other table loaded fine. The cause: the is_weekend field held the text values True/False, but the column was defined as a numeric flag, so MySQL silently rejected every row. The fix aligned the column type to the data:

ALTER TABLE dim_date MODIFY is_weekend VARCHAR(5);

Re-running then loaded all 731 rows. A small but realistic example of diagnosing the root cause of a failed load rather than guessing.

Demonstrates: data loading, troubleshooting a failed import, and resolving a data-type mismatch.

Step 3 — Analysis in SQL

With the data loaded, analytical queries answered the five business questions, using joins across fact and dimension tables, aggregation, ordering, and window functions where share-of-total was needed.

1 · Volume & demand

Established the headline flows (≈32.8M barrels of crude in, ≈255M litres of product out over 24 months) and broke demand down by product, customer segment, and month. HSD accounted for ~81% of outbound volume, ATF the remaining ~19%. The key finding came from the monthly trend: ATF showed clear, repeating seasonality, peaking in June, July, and December across both years — a direct input into inventory planning and supplier scheduling.

2 · Supplier & transporter reliability

Reliability was measured using OTIF (On-Time-In-Full), the standard supply-chain metric, supported by average delay days, with suppliers banded into tiers via a CASE statement. North Sea Resources was the most reliable supplier at 86% OTIF; the weakest spot-market supplier fell to ~75%. Pipeline transport led at ~89% OTIF while marine vessel shipping was both the least reliable (~75%) and slowest to recover — consistent with marine logistics' real exposure to weather and port delays.

3 · Logistics & delivery efficiency

Lead times were calculated directly from the date fields with DATEDIFF rather than a pre-built column, then delay rates analysed by product, route, and mode. Inbound crude ran ~14 days average lead time with ~11% of shipments delayed. The most actionable finding: one outbound delivery lane carried a delay rate close to double the others — the clear first target for improvement.

4 · Cost

Connected logistics to money on both sides: crude spend (volume × unit cost) inbound, revenue (volume × unit price) outbound, with freight tracked separately. Two commercial findings stood out: spend differences between suppliers were driven by volume, not unit price (per-barrel costs sat in a narrow band), reframing where procurement leverage lies; and freight ran at a very low percentage of revenue, indicating an efficient distribution operation.

5 · Inventory health

Measured through days of cover and a stockout-risk flag, analysed by location and product. Around a fifth of monthly snapshots fell into stockout-risk territory, concentrated at one depot. ATF ran short more often than HSD despite being the smaller-volume product — connecting back to the Question 1 seasonality: ATF demand spikes in summer and December, exactly when its stock comes under pressure. The recommendation: flex ATF stock policy with the season rather than holding it flat.

Conclusion

One connected story

The project delivers an end-to-end view of the refinery supply chain, from crude arriving at the gate to product reaching the customer, answering all five questions with evidence:

  • Volume & demand is dominated by HSD, with ATF showing clear seasonal peaks.
  • Reliability varies meaningfully by supplier and transporter, with spot-market supply and marine transport carrying the most risk.
  • Delivery efficiency is strong overall, with one route standing out as a clear improvement target.
  • Cost is driven by volume rather than unit price, and distribution runs efficiently on freight.
  • Inventory risk concentrates in aviation fuel during peak-demand periods, pointing to a seasonal stocking policy.

Together these turn a raw logistics dataset into a set of decisions an operations team could act on tomorrow.
