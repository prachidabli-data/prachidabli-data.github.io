Statistical Modelling Project I: Student Exam Performance

A demonstration of core statistical techniques (distributions, correlation and regression) applied to a synthetic student dataset to test hypotheses about what actually relates to exam performance.

Python pandas / NumPy SciPy statsmodels Matplotlib / Seaborn

320 synthetic student records · 6 variables · distribution, correlation, regression & hypothesis testing

This project was originally completed during my undergraduate degree. The original coursework files are no longer available, so this page presents a faithful rebuild: a new synthetic dataset generated in Python with the same realistic structure, run through the same statistical techniques, so every number, chart and table on this page comes from code you can see and re-run.

Objective

The question

The brief behind this piece of coursework was to take a dataset and demonstrate a full statistical workflow: describe it, understand how its variables are distributed, test whether variables relate to one another, and build a model that explains an outcome, with every step supported by formal hypothesis tests rather than eyeballed patterns.

The illustrative question chosen: what is actually associated with a student's exam score, and does it hold up once you run the numbers, or is it just intuition? Six variables were modelled for 320 students: weekly study hours, attendance rate, sleep hours, prior GPA, extracurricular hours, and study method (self-study, group study, or tutoring).

  • Is the exam score roughly normally distributed, and how spread out is it?
  • Which variables correlate meaningfully with exam performance, and which don't?
  • How well does study time alone predict score, and how much better does a multi-variable model do?
  • Do students with high attendance score significantly higher, on average, than those with low attendance?
  • Does study method (self-study vs. group study vs. tutoring) make a statistically significant difference?
The Data

A realistic synthetic dataset

The dataset is fully synthetic, generated programmatically in Python (NumPy) with a fixed random seed so every result on this page is reproducible. It was deliberately built with realistic statistical structure rather than a perfect textbook relationship:

  • Exam score is generated as a weighted combination of the predictors below plus random noise, so relationships exist, but are realistically noisy rather than deterministic.
  • A sleep penalty kicks in only below 6 hours a night, giving a mild non-linear effect to investigate.
  • Study method groups are unevenly sized (self-study is most common, tutoring least), as they would be in a real cohort.
  • Several predictors (sleep, extracurricular hours) are included specifically because they turn out not to matter much: a deliberate check against assuming every variable is significant.

Methodology

From raw data to tested hypotheses

Step 1: Descriptive statistics & distribution

Before testing relationships, the shape of the outcome variable needs checking: regression and t-tests lean on assumptions about the data's distribution. Exam scores were plotted and tested for normality with a Shapiro-Wilk test.

Demonstrates: descriptive statistics, distribution visualisation, formal normality testing.

With p = 0.497 (> .05), the Shapiro-Wilk test does not reject normality: the exam-score distribution is close to bell-shaped, with skewness near zero. That justifies using regression and t-tests, both of which assume approximately normal, continuous outcomes.

Step 3: Simple linear regression

Study hours showed the strongest single correlation, so it was modelled first on its own: exam_score ~ study_hours_per_week, fitted by ordinary least squares (OLS) in statsmodels.

Each extra hour of weekly study is associated with roughly 1.4 additional exam points. But study hours alone explain under 27% of the variation in scores: a clear sign that other factors matter too, which motivates the multiple regression below.

Step 4: Multiple linear regression

All five numeric predictors plus study method (as a categorical dummy) were entered into a multiple OLS regression, to see how much of exam score they jointly explain and which stay significant once the others are controlled for.

model = smf.ols(
    "exam_score ~ study_hours_per_week + attendance_rate + sleep_hours "
    "+ prior_gpa + extracurricular_hours + C(study_method)",
    data=df,
).fit()

Hypothesis Testing

Do the differences hold up statistically?

1 · Attendance: high vs. low (Welch's two-sample t-test)

H₀: mean exam score is equal for students attending ≥85% of sessions vs. those attending less. Comparing 126 high-attendance students (mean = 76.13, sd = 9.48) against 194 lower-attendance students (mean = 69.38, sd = 10.08):

p < .001

+6.74

[4.56, 8.92]

The gap is both statistically significant and practically meaningful: a nearly 7-point average advantage for consistent attendance, with a 95% CI that comfortably excludes zero.

2 · Study method (one-way ANOVA)

H₀: mean exam score is equal across self-study, group-study, and tutoring students. Group means were close (self-study 71.2, group study 72.1, tutoring 74.7), so this needed a formal test rather than a visual read.

F = 2.28
One-way ANOVA F-statistic
p = 0.104
p-value Fail to reject H₀

Despite tutoring students scoring highest on average, the difference between groups is not statistically significant at the 5% level. This is a genuinely useful negative result: on this data, it would be overreaching to claim study method drives exam performance. The apparent gap could plausibly be down to chance, at least at this sample size.

Demonstrates: two-sample t-testing, confidence intervals, one-way ANOVA, and correctly reporting a non-significant result rather than over-claiming.

Conclusion

What the numbers actually support

  • Exam scores are approximately normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.497), supporting the use of regression and t-tests.
  • Study hours, attendance, and prior GPA are all significantly correlated with exam score; sleep and extracurricular commitment are not, at least not on their own.
  • A simple regression on study hours alone explains ~27% of score variation; a multiple regression combining the significant predictors nearly doubles that, to ~48%.
  • Students attending ≥85% of sessions score ~6.7 points higher on average, a statistically significant and practically meaningful gap (Welch's t-test, p < .001).
  • Study method does not show a statistically significant effect (ANOVA, p = 0.104): a reminder that an eye-catching pattern in a bar chart still needs a formal test before it's treated as a finding.

The overall workflow (distribution check, correlation screening, simple then multiple regression, and formal hypothesis testing) is the same structure used in every later analytics project in this portfolio, just applied here to its purest, most mathematical form.
